Project-I · Review 2

Teaching a security system to refuse

An intrusion detector that keeps learning can be taught the wrong thing. This project puts an authorization step in front of the learning, and gives it one input the attacker cannot reach.

Dataset N-BaIoT, Danmini Doorbell Features 115 Rounds 80 Arms 4 Headline 0.200 → 1.000
01 · Summary

The whole project in one page

A network intrusion detector is trained once and then deployed. Networks change, so a frozen detector slowly goes wrong and starts crying wolf. The standard fix is to let it keep learning from live traffic.

That fix creates a new weakness. An attacker who knows the detector keeps learning does not have to sneak past it — they can retrain it. By nudging their traffic a tiny bit more normal-looking each time, they can walk the detector's idea of "normal" over to cover their own attack. Nothing looks alarming at any single step. This is called poisoning, and unlike ordinary evasion the damage stays behind after the attack stops.

Every existing defence against this inspects the training data to decide whether to trust it. That is the flaw we build on: the attacker controls that data, so the attacker controls the evidence.

Our system inserts an explicit authorization gate between "the detector noticed something changed" and "the detector learns from it". The gate scores the traffic three ways — but because all three scores come from traffic the attacker is shaping, it also consults one signal that does not: where the traffic is going. If the destination is known-bad, the update is refused no matter how healthy the traffic evidence looks.

We ran four detectors side by side on identical real traffic, differing in exactly one property each, through a paced poisoning campaign. Then we asked the question that actually matters: afterwards, can each detector still catch an obvious, full-strength repeat of the attack it was originally trained to catch?

The gate that checked only the traffic was fooled, and ended up as damaged as the one with no gate at all. The gate that additionally consulted an independent channel refused every proposal and finished with its detection ability completely intact.

Say this honestly

Our reputation channel is simulated. The dataset carries no destination or reputation field at all, so we key it to ground-truth traffic origin. It is not computed from the traffic features — which is what preserves its independence in principle — but it is closer to an oracle than a real threat-intelligence feed would be. The defensible claim is what an authorization step achieves once an independent signal exists, not that we detect the attacker.

02 · Motivation

Why both obvious options fail

Option A — never update

Train once, freeze forever. Cannot be poisoned, because it cannot learn.

But networks move. Devices get firmware updates, usage changes with the season, new services appear. The decision boundary slowly stops matching reality, false alarms climb, and operators start ignoring the alerts. A detector nobody listens to protects nothing.

Option B — always update

Keep learning from live traffic. Tracks the network as it changes.

But now the attacker has a lever a frozen detector never gave them. Instead of evading the boundary, they move it. And because the detector's whole notion of normal is now attacker-influenced, the damage outlives the attack.

The interesting observation is that in a typical adaptive pipeline, the decision to accept an update is never actually made. A change detector fires and the model retrains. Nothing sits between the two. There is no place in the architecture where the question "should this update be permitted?" is asked as a separate question — and so no place where the answer could be recorded, audited, or refused.

That missing step is the whole project.

03 · Threat model

Who we are defending against

Being precise here matters, because a defence is only meaningful against a stated adversary.

Who
An attacker who has compromised a device on the network and wants to keep using it for command-and-control traffic without being detected.
What they know
That the detector adapts. This is white-box with respect to the architecture: they know updates happen and roughly how often.
What they control
The traffic their compromised device emits — its timing, its volume, its statistical shape. Everything the detector measures.
What they don't control
Where their traffic has to go. The command-and-control server is theirs, and it is a fixed, known-bad destination. They can disguise the traffic; they cannot change who it is talking to.
Their strategy
Patience. Rather than a single loud attack, pace the malicious traffic so its statistical signature migrates gradually toward the benign region, one small step per adaptation window.
Their goal
Not to evade one detection — to get the detector to learn that this traffic is normal, so the evasion becomes permanent.

In the standard taxonomy this is a causative, targeted attack: causative because it manipulates training rather than merely dodging inference, targeted because it aims at one specific behaviour rather than degrading the detector generally.

The key asymmetry

The attacker controls everything the traffic looks like, but not where it goes. Every defence we surveyed spends its budget on the first half. This project spends it on the second.

04 · The idea

Authorization, not better detection

The instinct when facing poisoning is to build a smarter detector — a better statistic that can tell poisoned windows from genuine drift. We deliberately did not do that, because we do not think it can work.

Any statistic computed from the traffic is a measurement of something the attacker is actively shaping. Make the statistic cleverer and the attacker adapts to it; they have unlimited patience and full control of the input. This is not a question of finding the right feature. It is structural.

So instead of asking "is this traffic malicious?" — a detection question — we ask "should this model be permitted to learn from this?" — an authorization question. The reframing buys three things:

  • A place to stand. Authorization is a decision, so it can be recorded with a reason, audited afterwards, and refused. Detection scores blend into the model; decisions do not.
  • A reason to consult outside evidence. A detector is expected to work from the traffic. An authorizer is not — it can legitimately demand a second opinion from a channel the subject of the decision does not control.
  • A safe default. Refusing an update is cheap. The model simply stays as it was. There is no symmetric "safe default" for a detection system.

The analogy we find useful: this is the difference between a better lie detector and a second signature. You do not improve a signature by studying handwriting harder; you require a second, independent one.

05 · Architecture

Three tiers, one route to the model

The system is organised so there is exactly one path from a proposed update to the model, and that path runs through the gate. Two components sit deliberately outside that path: the reputation channel, which feeds in from the side and is never derived from the traffic, and the decision log, which receives a copy of every decision and returns nothing.

06 · The mechanism

Exactly what the gate computes

Three signals are measured from the window being proposed. All three live on a 0-to-1 scale, where higher means "looks more trustworthy".

AAttribution
Is this change heading the same way as changes we already approved? Formally, the cosine angle between this window's shift away from the trusted baseline and a running average of previously authorized shift directions. Returns a neutral 0.5 when nothing has been approved yet, so it never invents a score.
PTopology
Is the traffic varied, the way a real device's usage is — or narrow and repetitive, the way a single command-and-control channel is? Measured as the normalised entropy of a nine-bin histogram. A stand-in, because this dataset ships no address information.
SStability
Is the change gradual, or did it jump? One over one-plus the distance moved since the previous window. This is the signal a patient attacker maximises for free — going slowly costs them nothing — which is precisely why it proves nothing alone.
RReputation
Is this traffic going somewhere known to be bad? Not computed from the feature vector at all. This is the only input the attacker cannot move by reshaping traffic.
T = 0.40·A + 0.30·P + 0.30·S — the trust score

authorize = FALSE if R < 0.30 (the veto)
authorize = (T ≥ 0.55) otherwise

Two lines. The first is the contribution; the second is what everyone already does. Delete the first line and you have the "checks traffic only" baseline — which is exactly how we built that comparison arm, so the two differ in one branch and nothing else.

What happens after a decision

on ACCEPT: model.learn(window)
r ← 0.7·r + 0.3·shift — reference direction moves
b ← window_mean — baseline moves

on REFUSE: — nothing. Not the model, not r, not b.

That asymmetry looks like a detail and turns out to be central. Section 13 shows what happens to a gate that lacks it.

07 · Design decision

The ruler has to be nailed down

All three traffic signals are geometric — they measure angles and distances between vectors. Geometry needs a coordinate system, and the coordinate system here is a scaling fitted to what normal traffic looks like.

The question is when to fit it. The convenient answer is "keep it up to date, refit as new data arrives". That answer is wrong, and dangerously so: if the measuring frame is refitted on live windows, then an attacker who controls the live windows controls the frame. They would be able to move the ruler and then measure themselves against it.

So the feature space is fitted once, on calibration data, and then frozen for the life of the run. It is a trust boundary, not a preprocessing convenience. The implementation deliberately exposes no way to refit it.

z = ( sign(x) · log(1 + |x|) − μ_calibration ) / σ_calibration

The signed logarithm compresses a huge dynamic range while keeping direction. It is signed rather than absolute because 20 of the 115 columns are correlations and covariances that go negative — and a correlation of −0.9 and one of +0.9 are opposite behaviours that must not be folded together.

08 · Experimental design

Four arms, one pipeline

Four detectors run through a single orchestrator on byte-identical data, with an identical change trigger. Each differs from its neighbour in exactly one property, so any difference in outcome is attributable.

ArmWhat it doesRole in the comparison
Never learnsFrozen after calibration Safety ceiling — cannot be poisoned because it cannot learn
Learns from everythingAccepts every proposal Damage floor — what happens with no gate at all
Checks traffic onlyAccepts if T ≥ 0.55 The state of the art: evidence-based gating, no outside signal
OursSame, plus the reputation veto The proposed mechanism
A detail that makes it a controlled experiment

The change detector watches the error stream of the arm that never updates. That means all four arms receive the identical trigger at the identical moment, and no arm's own bad decisions can corrupt when the others are asked to adapt. If each arm ran its own detector, they would diverge for reasons unrelated to the gate.

09 · The data

Real traffic, checked before use

N-BaIoT is a public dataset of real traffic captured from commercial IoT devices, including devices genuinely infected with the Mirai and Gafgyt botnets. Each record is 115 pre-extracted statistical features, so no packet parsing is needed.

FileRecordsFeatures MissingInfiniteRole

Column ordering is identical across all files, which we verified rather than assumed. We also measured two things that shaped the experiment:

  • The benign file is stationary. Distance between its first and tenth decile blocks is 0.16 standard deviations, while neighbouring blocks differ by 0.30 to 0.74. The endpoints are closer together than adjacent blocks are — so splitting this file in half produces no drift to detect. That finding forced us to fetch a second device.
  • The two devices are genuinely different. Danmini benign to Ennio benign is 5.7 standard deviations in the calibrated space — about thirty-six times the within-file wobble. Both endpoints are real, benign, clean-reputation traffic.
10 · What nearly went wrong

The defect that produced plausible nonsense

This section is included because it is the most instructive thing that happened, and because the failure was silent.

Raw N-BaIoT feature values span roughly nineteen orders of magnitude. Standard deviations run from 0.0066 to 3.2×1016; individual maxima reach 5.67×1017. Feeding those raw vectors into geometric evidence functions produces two failures at once:

  • Stability dies. The distance between window means is 5.79×1016, so S = 1/(1+that) = 1.7×10-17. Effectively zero, in every round, for every arm. Thirty per cent of the trust budget was permanently dead.
  • Attribution becomes a one-feature measurement. Five jitter-variance columns accounted for 71.8, 11.6, 6.6, 5.4 and 4.6 per cent of the shift vector's squared norm — 100.0 per cent between them. The nominal "cosine similarity across 115 features" was really measuring one column.

Why this matters more than the fix

A genuinely benign window scored 0.5295 against a 0.55 threshold and was refused. Had we not caught it, nothing would ever have been authorized, all four arms would have silently collapsed onto the frozen arm, and the experiment would have produced a confident, tidy, completely empty result. The pipeline would have run to completion without a single error.

It was found by evaluating the evidence functions directly on real windows and reading the numbers — not by looking at end-to-end accuracy, which looked fine.

11 · The experiment

Four phases of a poisoning campaign

400 calibration records, then 80 rounds of 20 records each, scored prequentially — every record is predicted before the model is allowed to train on it, so no label ever leaks backwards.

Rounds 1–20
Normal
Clean benign traffic. Reputation 1.0. All four arms agree; nothing is happening.
Rounds 21–50
Poisoning
Real Mirai records are walked gradually toward real benign records. Every endpoint is genuine captured traffic; only the schedule is imposed. Reputation stays at 0.05 the entire time — the destination never changed.
Rounds 51–70
Fully disguised
The attack now sits entirely inside the benign region.
Rounds 71–80
Repeat attack
A loud, full-strength, unmodified repeat of the exact attack the model was calibrated on. This phase decides the result.
Why the pacing is done in log space

Interpolating in raw feature space is dominated by those 1017-scale columns: the trajectory stays visually "attack" for almost its entire length and then collapses at the very end. That is a sudden attack with a long flat prefix — not a slow one, and not our threat model. Pacing is therefore done in the same signed-log space the detector effectively perceives, then mapped back to real-scale values.

Why every arm scores zero from round 36 to 70

This will be asked, so it is worth stating plainly: this is not a failure of any arm. By round 36 the paced interpolation has carried the attack traffic across the decision boundary. By phase three the attack records are numerically identical to benign records — we verified this to a maximum relative difference of 4.7×10-16 — while still carrying an attack label.

No detector that inspects only traffic can score above zero there. Not ours, and not the frozen arm, which never updated at all. That is why overall accuracy is a poor headline for this experiment and repeat-attack detection is the number that means something.

Round 40 is the controlled comparison

Look at the first two rows. Every single evidence value is identical — A, P, S and T all match to three decimals, because neither gate had accepted anything yet and so both still held the calibration baseline. T = 0.573 cleared the 0.55 threshold.

The traffic-only gate accepted. Ours refused, because R = 0.05 fell below the 0.30 veto line. Nothing whatsoever except the independent channel distinguishes those two decisions. That is the contribution, isolated to a single row.

An unauthorized update is self-reinforcing

Now look at rounds 72 to 75, where the two gates no longer agree on the evidence. This is the finding we did not go looking for.

Having accepted at round 40, the traffic-only gate advanced its own baseline and initialised its reference direction — using a poisoned window. Later poisoned windows then resembled that poisoned reference, so its attribution score climbed from the neutral 0.500 to 0.956, 0.753, 0.770 and 0.777, dragging T above threshold every time.

Our gate's attribution stayed pinned at exactly 0.500 for the entire run, because its reference direction was never initialised: it never authorized anything.

The real lesson

Accepting one bad update made the next one easier to accept. The corruption compounds. This is why the design rule that a refusal changes absolutely nothing — not the model, not the baseline, not the reference direction — matters more than the specific threshold values do. It is what stops the feedback loop, and it is why our arm finishes numerically identical to the frozen arm rather than merely better than the ungated one.

14 · Negative result

The experiment that produced nothing

The second scenario was meant to demonstrate the complementary property: that the gate does not obstruct legitimate adaptation. It did not work, and we are reporting it as-is.

ArmAccuracyPost-drift AcceptedChange windows
All four arms, identical0.99790.993 0 / 700

We supplied a real benign-to-benign shift of 5.7 standard deviations by moving from one device to another. No update was ever proposed, because no change was ever detected.

The reason is structural. The change detector watches an error stream. A benign-to-benign shift, however large in feature space, does not cross the boundary between benign and Mirai traffic — so the frozen detector kept classifying correctly at 0.998 and produced no error signal at all. Nothing degraded, so nothing was proposed, so the gate was never asked to decide.

Two readings, and we do not pick one

It is correct behaviour. A detector that is still working does not need to adapt, and adapting anyway would be gratuitous risk.

It is the wrong trigger. A distribution-based change detector watching the input rather than the error would have fired, and the scenario would have exercised the gate as intended.

Settling it requires an experiment we did not run. It is first on the future-work list. On the synthetic stream, where the drift does degrade the frozen model, our gate authorizes on 13 of 70 rounds — exactly as many as the traffic-only gate — which is evidence that the veto does not over-refuse clean traffic.

15 · Contribution

What is new, and what is not

Not new

  • The streaming classifier — standard, used as published
  • The change detector — standard, used unmodified
  • Poisoning as an attack concept — well established since 2006
  • Slow, paced poisoning — demonstrated against spam filters in 2008
  • The idea of gating an update — prior evidence-based gating exists

New here

  • Authorization as an explicit, recorded, refusable architectural stage
  • A deciding input that is not a function of the data being judged
  • The no-residue rule: a refusal changes nothing at all
  • Measuring durable damage via a post-attack repeat test
  • The self-reinforcement finding: one bad update begets the next

Stated as one sentence: every prior defence judges a proposed update using evidence drawn from the channel the attacker controls; this work adds a deciding input drawn from outside that channel, and measures what it is worth.

The honest framing of the strength of that claim: we demonstrate a failure mode and a mechanism that addresses it. We do not quantify a failure rate, because that would require the sensitivity analysis we have not yet run.

16 · Limitations

Everything wrong with this study

  • The reputation channel is an oracle. Keyed to ground truth, it never errs. A real feed would be stale, incomplete and sometimes wrong, and we have measured nothing about how the gate behaves when it is. This is the single largest threat to external validity.
  • One configuration. One device, one attack family, one classifier, one untuned parameter set, one poisoning schedule. No sensitivity analysis.
  • Small denominators. The headline rests on ten rounds within a single run. No significance test is computed, and none would be supportable.
  • The threshold was never calibrated. 0.55 was chosen before any real result was seen. Round 40 cleared it at 0.573 — a slightly higher threshold would have refused that update and the traffic-only arm might have survived intact.
  • No automated tests. Claims were verified by direct measurement, but there is no regression suite, so future changes could break something silently.
  • The attacker's pacing is our construction. Real capture files are not paced slowly; we imposed the schedule between real endpoints.
  • The topology signal is a stand-in. With no address data available, it approximates communication diversity from feature spread.
17 · Implementation

How the code is arranged

Online detector
Wraps the streaming pipeline and owns the change detector. Exposes one operation for prequential scoring and another for applying an authorized batch.
Feature space
The calibration-fitted, thereafter frozen transform. Exposes fit and transform, and deliberately provides no way to refit — the trust boundary is enforced by the interface.
Evidence functions
The three signals. Stateless by design; all mutable state lives in the orchestrator.
Trust gate
Holds weights and thresholds, returns an immutable decision record carrying the verdict, the three evidence values, the score, the reputation and a human-readable reason.
Orchestrator
Owns four detector instances, two gate instances, and the per-gate baseline and reference direction. Runs the round loop.
Stream builders
Construct the scenarios from real capture files, including the log-space paced interpolation.
Replay interface
Steps through a recorded run one round at a time, showing classification, evidence and the authorization decision as they occurred.

Every decision the system makes is persisted with its reason, so any round can be reconstructed and explained without re-running the pipeline.

18 · Next

Future work, in priority order

  1. A distribution-based change trigger alongside the error-based one, so benign drift that does not degrade the detector still prompts a review. This is what turns the null result into a demonstration.
  2. A degraded reputation channel. Measure the gate under a feed that is stale, incomplete or wrong. This is the only route by which the present oracle result becomes an engineering claim.
  3. An elastic-weight-consolidation baseline, to separate refusing a bad update from resisting forgetting — a distinction we currently assert but do not measure.
  4. Threshold and weight sensitivity, converting a demonstrated failure mode into a quantified failure rate.
  5. Breadth — more devices, more attack families, a non-linear classifier.
19 · Reference

Glossary

Prequential
Predict first, then train, for every record. Ensures no label is ever seen before the prediction it would have influenced.
Concept drift
The distribution the model sees changing after deployment.
Poisoning
An attack on training rather than on inference. Manipulating what a model learns rather than dodging what it knows.
Causative attack
The formal name for poisoning: the adversary influences the training data. Contrast with an exploratory attack, which only probes the deployed model.
Change detector
An algorithm that watches a stream of values and signals when its distribution has shifted. Ours watches the frozen arm's error stream.
Out-of-band
Arriving through a different channel from the one under scrutiny. The property that makes reputation useful here.
Veto
A refusal that overrides the other evidence rather than being weighed against it.
Repeat-attack detection
Our headline metric: after the campaign ends, how often does the detector still catch a full-strength repeat of the attack it was calibrated on? Measures durable damage rather than momentary evasion.
N-BaIoT
A public dataset of real traffic from commercial IoT devices, including genuinely botnet-infected ones. 115 pre-extracted features per record.
Mirai
A well-known IoT botnet. Its scan traffic is the attack used throughout this study.
